## What I look at, without a price list

People ask what affects the value before they get in touch. I do not publish prices. Nothing on this site is an offer for a particular album. What I can tell you is what I look at, because that is what I hope to find when I am standing in the room.

Rock from the 1960s and 1970s is the music I have loved longest, and I mean any type of it: psychedelic, blues rock, hard rock, progressive, folk rock, heavy rock, early heavy metal, garage and freakbeat. The records wanted first are late 1960s and early 1970s rock. Albums, singles and box sets, in their sleeves if you still have them.

Original UK pressings, gatefold sleeves and the old label designs make me smile. Island pink label, Vertigo swirl, Harvest, Charisma, Deram, Atlantic plum, Parlophone and Immediate are names I like to see. You do not have to check each label before you write. If you happen to notice one, mention it. If not, I will see it when I come. Do not pull out only the famous names. I would rather see the shelf they came from.

The paper belongs with the disc. Inner sleeves, lyric sheets, posters, booklets and the outer box of a box set should stay together. Leave a gatefold closed. If a poster is folded inside, leave it there. If an insert has fallen out and you know which sleeve it came from, put it back. If you are not sure, keep the loose paper in the same box. Do not throw it away because it looks like scrap. A split corner, a ring mark, or a spine gone soft is still part of the record. Do not throw out a tatty sleeve, and do not put sticky tape across a tear.

Played copies are welcome. A record that has been on a turntable for years is still a record I want to hold. Please do not apologise for the condition, and please do not hide the worn ones. I will look at plain, played, well-loved copies. Dust is nothing to apologise for.

Tell me about the whole collection, not only the rock. I collect all kinds of music for my own shelves: soul and Motown, country, folk, reggae, and mixed boxes from the loft. I may be interested in buying more than the 1960s and 1970s rock. A good record is never turned away. If you are not sure they are my sort, I would rather see them than have you decide for me.

If you want to keep some, keep them, and say so. Put them in another room, or name them in the email, and I will look at the rest. Do not make a pile for the tip before I have been. Please leave the collection together until I have looked.

## Caring for the vinyl until I arrive

You do not need a special cabinet, and you do not need to buy new sleeves. I would rather see the records as they have been kept. Keep the paper they already have.

When a record has to be lifted, keep your fingers on the rim or on the label, and off the grooves. You do not have to take them out of the sleeves to show me. The Library of Congress notes on [care, handling and storage of audio-visual materials](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/care/record.html) say to handle LPs by the edge and the label only, to store discs upright, and to keep a home collection cool and relatively dry, out of attics and basements, away from radiators and out of direct light. That is sound advice while the records wait. You do not have to measure the humidity, and you do not have to finish a storage project before I visit.

If the records are already standing upright, leave them. A short lean is nothing to worry about. A flat pile, with something heavy on top, is harder on the sleeves and on the discs. If they have been stored flat for years, tell me, and leave them. If they are already in boxes from a move, leave them in the boxes. Stand the boxes so the records are upright inside, when that is easy. Do not unpack the lot onto the floor.

Full sun in a window, a radiator, a loft that gets very hot, or a cellar that feels wet are poor places for them to wait, if you can move them without a large job. A cool, dry room is enough.

Please do not deep-clean a record with household products. Washing-up liquid, window spray, alcohol, or anything from under the sink can do more harm than years of playing. Do not wipe the playing surface with a tissue, a paper towel, or the sleeve of a shirt. If a record looks dull, leave it dull.
